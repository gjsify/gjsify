When your window has several peer pages and shows one at a time, put the pages in a
**view stack** and give the user a **switcher** to pick between them. Five switchers are
on this page, and all of them adapt to the window: a wide desktop gets the full switcher
and a narrow phone gets a collapsed one.

<MoreInfo title="What the preview does not show">
The previews run the browser port, so gestures Libadwaita drives natively
(drag-to-reorder tabs, the tab bar's auto-hide, cross-fade transitions) can be simplified
here. Click a switcher button, a tab or a carousel dot to move between pages. The
carousel's own gestures are not simplified — see below.
</MoreInfo>

## View Switcher

The standard switcher: one segmented control whose buttons each carry a page's
icon and label, driving a paired **view stack**. Its **policy** decides the
button layout. `wide` puts the icon and label side by side; `narrow` stacks the
icon above the label so the bar fits a slim window. An adaptive layout normally
swaps between the two on a breakpoint.

## View Switcher Bar

The **narrow-window** half of the same pattern. When the window is too slim for a
switcher in the header bar, put one at the bottom edge instead — that is a view switcher
bar, pinned into a [toolbar view](/gjsify/adwaita/layout/#toolbar-view)'s bottom slot
and bound to the same stack.

<MoreInfo title="`reveal` is a request, not a state">
A stack with fewer than two pages stays collapsed however loudly it is asked, so a bar
over a one-page stack simply does not appear.
</MoreInfo>

## Tab View

Reach for a tab view when the pages are not fixed. Unlike a view switcher, tabs are meant
to be added, closed and reordered while the app runs. The **tab bar** draws one chip per
page, each with a title and a close button, and raises the selected chip onto the view
background.

<MoreInfo title="Autohide">
Turn on **autohide** and the bar disappears once a single page is left, so a one-tab
document looks chrome-free.
</MoreInfo>

## Tab Button

A button that stands in for a tab bar when there is no room for one. It shows
how many pages its **view** holds, and lights a dot when a page you are not
looking at needs attention. The number gets smaller from ten pages and gives way
to a glyph at a hundred. Pair it with an action that opens a tab overview, and
listen to `clicked` to do so.

## Tab Overview

The **overview** is every page at once: a grid of thumbnails over the tab view, with the
page title under each one and its own close button on it. It is meant to be the direct
child of the window, with the tab view as its **child** — opening it covers that view
and closing it gives it back.

The header bar counts the open pages (**3 Tabs**, **1 Tab** for a single page) and
carries the search button; the search matches titles, tooltips and each page's
**keyword**, so a browser can make its URLs findable. Turn **enable-search** off and
the button goes, leaving a bar that shows nothing worth showing — which is also what
happens when the secondary menu is unset and both sides' title buttons are off.

<MoreInfo title="Pinned pages">
A **pinned** page gets its own grid above the ordinary one, and its thumbnail carries an
unpin mark instead of a close button — that is the arrangement the tab bar's narrow
chips come from. **Inverted** puts each thumbnail's close button at the leading edge and
its indicator at the trailing one, for a right-to-left layout.
</MoreInfo>

<MoreInfo title="What the thumbnails are not">
GTK paints each page into a texture and shows that picture. A browser cannot paint a
widget into a texture, so the portrait area shows the page's own title instead. Everything
around it — the card, the close button, the unpin mark, the attention dot, the indicator —
is the real thing. Thumbnails are not dragged to reorder here either: reordering goes
through the tab view's own API, the way the tab bar does it.
</MoreInfo>

## Inline View Switcher

When a full switcher is too wide for the space, use the inline one. It is built
from a linked toggle group and drives the same kind of view stack, but its
**display mode** lets each toggle show **labels**, **icons** or **both**. That
makes it fit a header bar or a tight toolbar.

## View Switcher Sidebar

The one Adw switcher built out of a **sidebar**, and the one whose rows can be
**grouped into sections**. Point it at a view stack and each page becomes a row with
its icon, its title and — when it asks — an unread **dot** or a **badge count**;
`starts-section` on a page opens a new group, headed by that page's `section-title`.

It is adaptive in the sidebar's sense: **mode="page"** turns it into a page of boxed
lists, which is what a `NavigationSplitView` wants for its sidebar pane once the window
is too narrow for a sidebar. Keep it in sidebar mode with an **OverlaySplitView**,
where the pane is still a sidebar when collapsed.

<MoreInfo title="Ordering of `activated`">
Activating a row selects the stack page **first** and only then emits `activated` —
that is the signal a split view reveals its content pane on, so a handler that read the
stack inside it would otherwise reveal the page you were leaving.
</MoreInfo>

## Carousel

For onboarding screens and galleries, use a carousel: pages sit side by side and the
user flicks, scrolls or clicks a **page indicator** to move between them. Two indicator
widgets bind to a carousel, **dots** (shown here) and **lines**. Both track the
fractional scroll position and grow the marker nearest the current page.

<MoreInfo title="The three gestures">
**Touch** and a **touchpad** scroll the carousel natively — the browser owns those,
momentum and snapping included. A **mouse drag** is the one the browser has no gesture
for, so it is implemented: press inside the carousel and pull, and the pages follow the
cursor. Where it lands is `Adw.SwipeTracker`'s decision, not the browser's — the drag's
velocity is projected and the nearest reachable page wins, so a short flick pages and a
slow half-drag settles back.
</MoreInfo>

<MoreInfo title="The three knobs">
The **scroll wheel** pages the carousel by default; set `allow-scroll-wheel="false"` to
leave wheel events to the page instead. **Mouse drag** is on by default;
`allow-mouse-drag="false"` leaves dragging to touch, as it does upstream. **Long swipes**
are off by default; turn them on to let one flick skip several pages — with the caveat
that a *touchpad* flick can already cross more than one page here. The setting is
applied (each page gets `scroll-snap-stop: always` when long swipes are off), but the
browser does not honour it for that gesture; GTK, which runs the same input through its
own tracker, does.
</MoreInfo>
